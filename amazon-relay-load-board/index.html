How the Amazon Relay Load Board Works

The Amazon Relay load board is where carriers find and book loads. Good loads get taken in seconds, which is why understanding how the board works and what you're looking at matters.

What the Load Board Is

The Amazon Relay load board is your direct connection to loads that Amazon Logistics needs moved. You log in at relay.amazon.com with your carrier credentials, see available loads, and book them. It's the only place Amazon posts loads for Relay carriers. Third-party load boards and brokers don't carry Amazon's freight.

When you sign in, you see a list of loads posted that day. Each load card shows the pickup location, delivery location, the load type, the posted rate, and other details you need to decide whether to book it.

Why Loads Vanish So Quickly

Good loads disappear within seconds of posting because the board attracts thousands of active carriers watching for work. The moment a profitable load lands, multiple carriers see it at once and one of them books it first. This is why many carriers refresh the load board constantly—they're trying to catch good loads before anyone else does.

A manual refresh means you walk away, come back, hit reload, and check what's new. But if you step away for five minutes, you miss dozens of refreshes and all the loads that posted in that window. That's why carriers use refreshers and auto-booker extensions to stay in the competition.

Reading a Load Card

Every load card on the board shows the same core information, though the layout varies depending on your device and which extension you're using.

Origin and Destination: The pickup location and drop-off location. These are what drive the rate and determine whether the load fits your dispatch plan.

Equipment Type: What the load is (box truck, dry van, reefer, container). Amazon accepts day cab and sleeper tractors, 26 ft and 16 ft box trucks with tuck-under liftgates, 48 ft and 53 ft dry van and reefer trailers, and 20/40/45 ft high-cube containers. Cargo vans are not supported.

Posted Rate: The dollar amount Amazon is paying for that load. This is the gross rate, before driver pay, fuel, maintenance, and deadhead. Many carriers now use Net Profit tools to see what you actually keep after costs.

Load Type: One-way or round-trip. One-way loads move freight in one direction. Round-trip loads are part of blocks—multi-load runs booked as a unit—or may include a return route. You can also see loads through Post A Truck, where you post your availability and get matched.

Filters and Search

Most carriers use the built-in filters to narrow down what they see. You can filter by load type (one-way vs. round-trip, blocks), equipment category, destination, and sometimes by origin. Filters save time because you don't have to look at every load posted.

If you're only moving dry vans, filtering out box truck loads means less scrolling. If you have a home hub and want work headed toward it, filtering by destination narrows the list. The more specific your filter, the faster you can scan for loads that match your operation.

How Rates Are Set

Amazon sets the rate for each load based on several factors: the season, the time of day, the origin, the equipment type, your booking history, and the day's load volume. The same lane posted at 6 a.m. might pay differently at noon because the volume and demand shifted.

This means two identical lanes can pay completely different amounts depending on when they post. Understanding that rates move with demand—and not taking it personally when a rate looks low—is part of the business.

Refreshing and Staying Competitive

Manual refreshing works if you're sitting at your computer all day, but most owner-operators can't camp on the load board. Refreshing every 30 seconds by hand isn't practical. A refresh extension solves this by automating the reload, typically at intervals of 1 to 5 seconds, with built-in jitter to avoid overwhelming the server.

The jitter—random variation added to the interval—keeps the server stable by preventing thousands of carriers from refreshing at exactly the same second. Without jitter, everyone hits at once; with it, requests spread out.

Once you have a refresher running, you can set an alert to ping you when a load matching your rules posts, or use a search highlight to spot matching loads visually, or set up an auto-booker to book them for you automatically if you've set a profit floor and max-books cap.

Load Types and Booking Methods

Spot Loads: One-way and round-trip loads you find and book yourself. Book, complete before 11:59 pm Saturday, and get paid Friday.

Blocks: Multi-load runs posted as a unit. You book the whole block and complete all the loads in it.

Short-Term Contracts: Standing work for a set term.

Post A Truck: You post your truck's availability, location, and willingness to move freight, and Amazon matches you with loads headed your way.

Each method works differently. Spot loads are quickest and give you the most control. Blocks commit you to multiple stops but often offer better rates. Contracts provide stability. Post A Truck is passive—you post and wait for Amazon to offer matches.

The Scorecard and Load Access

Your performance on the Relay load board is tracked in a scorecard that covers safety compliance, on-time performance, tender acceptance, and technology/app usage. Low scores can limit your access to loads.

If you accept a load and then cancel it, that counts against you. If you miss a pickup window, that counts. If your safety rating sags, Amazon can tighten your access to premium loads. If you believe a scorecard violation is wrong, dispute it as soon as you see it—Amazon decides the outcome—or work on the metrics to rebuild your score.

Getting Started

To use the load board, you need a carrier account with Amazon Relay. That requires a DOT number with interstate authority, active for at least 180 days; a valid MC number; a Satisfactory BASIC safety rating; insurance minimums (GL $1M per occurrence, auto liability $1M, cargo at least $100K); and the right equipment.

Once you're approved, log in at relay.amazon.com, sign in with your carrier credentials, and you'll see the load board. From there, the board is yours to use as-is, or you can add a refresher extension or booking tool to work smarter.

Frequently asked questions

What time do Amazon Relay loads post on the load board?

Loads post throughout the day as Amazon needs freight moved. There's no set time. The busiest periods tend to be early morning and around midday, but loads post anytime. This is why refreshing constantly helps you catch them.

Can I book Amazon loads from a third-party load board?

No. Amazon only posts loads on the Relay load board. Brokers and third-party platforms don't carry Amazon freight. You must use relay.amazon.com to find and book Amazon work.

Why do good Amazon Relay loads get booked so fast?

Thousands of Relay carriers watch the same load board. When a profitable load posts, multiple carriers see it in the same second. The fastest person to click gets it. This is why carriers use refresher extensions—to reload the board every few seconds instead of manually, so they catch good loads before other carriers do.

How do I know if an Amazon Relay rate is good?

Rates vary by lane, time of day, season, and volume. The same route can pay very differently hour to hour. The best way to judge a rate is to track what that lane has paid over the past 90 days, calculate your net profit after all costs (driver pay, fuel, maintenance, deadhead), and set a profit floor—don't book loads that don't clear it. Lane Intelligence tools show you the history; a profit calculator shows you the net dollars.

What loads can I book with different equipment?

Box truck loads are for 26 ft and 16 ft trucks with tuck-under liftgates. Tractor loads are for day cab and sleeper units moving 48/53 ft trailers or containers. The board filters by equipment type. Check which equipment you're registered with Relay and filter to loads that match it.

How often should I refresh the load board?

Manual refreshing every 30 seconds isn't practical. Most carriers use a refresh extension set to 1–5 second intervals with jitter. This keeps you in the hunt for loads without you staring at the screen. Set the refresh interval based on your tolerance for notification noise and your internet connection.
