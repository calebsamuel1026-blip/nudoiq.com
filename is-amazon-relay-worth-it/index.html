Is Amazon Relay Worth It?

Amazon Relay isn't right for everyone. It offers fast weekly pay and consistent load supply, but it demands speed, automation, and discipline on the scorecard. Here's an honest look at who it works for and who should look elsewhere.

The Case For Amazon Relay

Fast Weekly Pay You get paid every Friday, no exceptions. No waiting weeks for a broker to collect money and remit it. No invoices chasing or payment disputes with small shippers. Reliable, predictable cash flow is huge for carriers with tight margins or limited working capital.

Steady Load Supply Amazon has freight every single day. Unlike freight boards where you might hunt for loads during a slow period, Relay always has work available. As long as your scorecard is decent, you'll see loads. This predictability is especially valuable if you want to plan your week or commit to regular routes.

No Haggling Rates are posted. You accept or decline. No back-and-forth with dispatchers, no rate negotiations, no pressure to cut your rate. This saves time and mental energy. The clarity is worth something.

National Scale Amazon's load network spans the country. You're not limited to regional shippers or a single freight board. You can find loads to nearly anywhere Amazon ships.

Performance Transparency Your scorecard gives you clear feedback on what Amazon values: on-time delivery, safe driving, app responsiveness. You know exactly what hurts you and what helps you. Some carriers prefer this clarity over the mystery of traditional brokers.

The Case Against Amazon Relay

Speed Competition Good loads book within seconds. If you're manually refreshing the board every few minutes, you'll almost never catch premium freight. You need either hyper-responsiveness or an auto-refresh tool to stay competitive. This is not passive income; it's active speed competition.

Rate Volatility The same lane can pay very differently on different days. This unpredictability makes it hard to forecast income or plan operating margins. You have to evaluate every load individually and be prepared to decline most of them if they don't meet your profit floor.

Scorecard Pressure Amazon's performance metrics are strict. One missed pickup, one late delivery, or a few declined loads can tank your score and reduce your load access. The algorithm is not forgiving. If you struggle with on-time delivery or can't commit to high tender acceptance, Relay will punish you.

Deadhead Reality If you're not already positioned near a pickup, deadhead costs eat into profit. Box truck loads tend to be local, so deadhead is usually manageable. But tractor loads can post far away, and driving there empty wipes out margin.

Limited Negotiation If a load is priced below what you need, there's no way to negotiate. Amazon doesn't care about your fuel cost or maintenance budget. You decline and wait for the next load, or you accept a loss.

No Broker Relationship On a traditional freight board, you might build a relationship with a dispatcher who knows your equipment and calls you first with good loads. On Relay, you're anonymous. You compete for the same loads as everyone else. There's no personal advantage.

Rating and Review Visibility Amazon publishes your scorecard publicly within the Relay system. Carriers with low scores lose access to the best loads. Unlike traditional brokers where the relationship is somewhat private, Relay's metric-based system is completely transparent and tied to your access.

Who Relay Works For

Amazon Relay works best for carriers who:

  • Want predictability over maximum earnings. Fast weekly pay and steady work matter more than hunting for premium loads.
  • Are disciplined about on-time delivery. If you consistently pick up and drop on schedule, your scorecard stays strong and you keep load access.
  • Can use automation. Auto-refresh tools and auto-booking extensions remove the speed disadvantage and help you set rules (profit floor, lane preferences) instead of manually deciding every load.
  • Are positioned near Amazon facilities. If you're close to a major distribution center, deadhead is reasonable and loads are plentiful.
  • Run box trucks or smaller equipment. Relay has steady box truck work. If you own a sleeper and can run long-haul, traditional freight boards might pay better and offer more flexibility.
  • Want simple operations. No customer relations, no multi-week disputes, no contract negotiations. Book, haul, deliver, get paid.

Who Relay Doesn't Work For

Amazon Relay is a poor fit if you:

  • Need flexibility and don't accept a scorecard system. If you frequently need to reschedule, decline loads for personal reasons, or work part-time, Amazon's metrics will hurt you.
  • Can't operate on tight margins. If you need to negotiate rates or have high overhead, Relay's fixed pricing and rate volatility will squeeze you.
  • Don't have capital for automation. If you can't afford an auto-refresh tool or don't want to pay for one, you'll struggle competing against automated competitors.
  • Are far from Amazon distribution. If you're far from the nearest Amazon facility, deadhead costs make most loads unprofitable.
  • Prefer long-haul or specialized freight. Relay focuses on regional freight and Amazon's network. Specialized loads (hazmat, oversized, livestock) don't appear on Relay.
  • Want a consistent per-mile rate. If rate variability stresses you or makes forecasting impossible, Relay's dynamic pricing is a bad fit.

The Honest Trade-Off

Amazon Relay is a volume and consistency play, not a high-rate play. You trade rate negotiation for load predictability. You trade flexibility for fast pay and clear metrics. You trade personal relationships for algorithmic transparency.

If you're a carrier who wants to show up, book loads, deliver on time, and collect a paycheck every Friday without drama, Relay works. If you're a carrier who wants to chase premium loads, negotiate rates, and maintain relationships with brokers, Relay feels limiting.

How Automation Changes the Equation

Auto-refresh tools and auto-booking extensions significantly improve Relay's value proposition. With automation:

  • You catch premium loads you'd otherwise miss (removes the speed disadvantage).
  • You set rules (profit floor, lane preferences) and let the tool do the repetitive work.
  • You reduce manual decision fatigue and focus on exceptions.
  • You can run multiple trucks from the same dashboard.

Without automation, Relay feels like a constant speed race and you'll miss good freight. With it, Relay becomes a manageable, repeatable system.

Final Answer: Is It Worth It?

For volume-focused carriers with strong on-time performance and access to Amazon facilities: Yes. The steady work and fast pay are valuable.

For carriers who need rate negotiation, flexibility, or long-haul work: No. You'll be frustrated by Relay's fixed pricing and regional focus.

For carriers who are currently on Relay but frustrated: Likely yes, but only if you adopt automation. An auto-refresh tool and rate/profit intelligence can transform Relay from a speed race to a manageable operation.

Frequently asked questions

Do most carriers make money on Amazon Relay?

Many carriers find Relay profitable, but profitability depends on your costs, location, equipment and discipline. Some carriers supplement Relay with other freight boards. Others run Relay exclusively. Your net profit per load determines whether it works for you.

Can I run Relay part-time?

Yes. You set your availability and accept or decline loads as you choose. But Amazon's scorecard tracks tender acceptance—declining too many loads can hurt your score and reduce load access.

What's the biggest pain point for Relay carriers?

The speed competition for good loads and rate variability are the two biggest complaints. Automation (auto-refresh and booking rules) solves the speed problem significantly.

Should I quit my broker and switch to Relay full-time?

Not necessarily. Many successful carriers use Relay as one channel alongside other boards. Relay is valuable for consistent work and fast pay, but not all lanes or shippers are on Relay.

Is Relay harder to manage than a traditional freight board?

No, it's actually simpler. With a freight board, you hunt for loads and negotiate. With Relay, loads are published, rates are fixed, and payment is automatic. The trade-off is less control and less negotiation.

How do I know if I have the right equipment for Relay?

Check the equipment requirements. Day cab, sleeper, box truck (16/26 ft with liftgate), reefer, dry van and containers all work. Cargo vans don't.
