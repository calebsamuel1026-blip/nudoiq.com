Is Amazon Relay Worth It?

Amazon Relay isn't right for everyone. It offers fast weekly pay and consistent load supply, but it demands speed, automation, and discipline on the scorecard. Here's an honest look at who it works for and who should look elsewhere.

NudoIQ · Published September 20, 2026 · Last updated October 2, 2026

Short answer

Amazon Relay can be worth it for carriers who want weekly pay, a steady supply of loads and listed spot prices with no haggling. The trade-offs are speed competition for good loads, rates that change by lane and hour, and a performance score that controls access to contracts and early loads. Whether it works depends on your costs.

The Case For Amazon Relay

Fast Weekly Pay Amazon pays weekly: loads completed by 11:59 pm Saturday are paid the following Friday, and invoices are generated automatically. No waiting weeks for a broker to collect money and remit it. No invoices chasing or payment disputes with small shippers. Reliable, predictable cash flow is huge for carriers with tight margins or limited working capital.

Steady Load Supply Amazon says the board has thousands of loads at any time, and volume nearly doubles during peak periods like Prime Day and the holidays. This predictability is especially valuable if you want to plan your week or commit to regular routes.

No Haggling Spot loads show an all-in price (base rate, fuel surcharge and tolls) and you book at that price. If you want to set your own number, Post A Truck takes a minimum rate per mile, auctions take bids on contracts, and Relay Assistant lets top-performing carriers name a price on eligible urgent loads.

National Scale Carriers hauling Amazon freight are spread across the country. In our FMCSA inspection data, California, Ohio, Illinois, Pennsylvania and Texas have the most carriers with Amazon-shipper inspections since 2025. Read it as a floor: an inspection isn't a load, and Amazon freight isn't always booked through Relay.

Performance Transparency Your grade gives you clear feedback on what Amazon measures: on-time, acceptance, app usage and disruption-free. Your score is the lowest of the four, and Amazon gives you a daily prediction of your upcoming score. You know exactly what hurts you and what helps you. Some carriers prefer this clarity over the mystery of traditional brokers.

The Case Against Amazon Relay

Speed Competition Good loads book within seconds. If you're manually refreshing the board every few minutes, you'll almost never catch premium freight. You need either hyper-responsiveness or an auto-refresh tool to stay competitive. This is not passive income; it's active speed competition.

Rate Volatility The same lane can pay very differently on different days. This unpredictability makes it hard to forecast income or plan operating margins. You have to evaluate every load individually and be prepared to decline most of them if they don't meet your profit floor.

Scorecard Pressure Your overall score is the lowest of four metrics, so one weak area sets the grade. Cancellations hurt more the closer they are to pickup, and dropping below C+ can cost you contracts you already booked. See our scorecard guide.

Deadhead Reality If you're not already positioned near a pickup, deadhead costs eat into profit. Loads can post far from where your truck sits, and driving there empty wipes out margin.

Limited Negotiation Most spot loads book at the listed price. Outside Post A Truck, auctions and Relay Assistant (for top-performing carriers), a load priced below what you need is a decline.

No Broker Relationship On a traditional freight board, you might build a relationship with a dispatcher who knows your equipment and calls you first with good loads. On Relay, your grade does that job: A+, A and B+ carriers see new spot loads first.

Rating and Review Visibility Your grade lives in your Relay account and decides what you can access. Lower grades lose access to contracts, auctions and early spot loads. Relay's system is metric-based and tied directly to your access.

Who Relay Works For

Amazon Relay works best for carriers who:

  • Want predictability. Weekly pay and a busy board matter more to you than hunting for the single best load.
  • Are disciplined about on-time delivery. If you consistently pick up and drop on schedule, your scorecard stays strong and you keep load access.
  • Can use automation. Auto-refresh tools and auto-booking extensions remove the speed disadvantage and help you set rules (profit floor, lane preferences) instead of manually deciding every load.
  • Are positioned near Amazon facilities. If you're close to a major distribution center, deadhead is reasonable and loads are plentiful.
  • Have the right equipment. Amazon says the majority of Relay loads are power only, so a tractor alone covers most work. Box trucks (16 or 26 ft with a liftgate) can also take one-way loads.
  • Want simple operations. No customer relations, no multi-week disputes, no contract negotiations. Book, haul, deliver, get paid.

Who Relay Doesn't Work For

Amazon Relay is a poor fit if you:

  • Often need to cancel booked loads. The acceptance metric tracks cancellations, including loads that expire, and the penalty grows the closer you are to pickup.
  • Can't absorb rate swings. Prices move with distance, season, region and load type, which is hard on high-overhead operations.
  • Don't want to use tools. Without an auto-refresh tool or alerts, you'll be reloading the board by hand against carriers who use them.
  • Are far from Amazon distribution. If you're far from the nearest Amazon facility, deadhead costs make most loads unprofitable.
  • Need specialized freight. Relay supports tractors, 16 and 26 ft box trucks, dry vans, reefers and high-cube containers, so freight that needs other equipment isn't a fit.
  • Want a consistent per-mile rate. If rate variability stresses you or makes forecasting impossible, Relay's dynamic pricing is a bad fit.

The Honest Trade-Off

Amazon Relay rewards consistency more than rate hunting. You trade a set rate for weekly pay and a busy board. You trade flexibility for clear metrics. You trade personal relationships for a metric-based grade.

If you're a carrier who wants to show up, book loads, deliver on time, and get paid weekly without chasing invoices, Relay can work. If you're a carrier who wants to chase premium loads, negotiate rates, and maintain relationships with brokers, Relay feels limiting.

How Automation Changes the Equation

Auto-refresh tools and auto-booking extensions change how Relay feels day to day. With automation:

  • You see new loads sooner instead of reloading by hand.
  • You set rules (profit floor, lane preferences) and let the tool do the repetitive work.
  • You reduce manual decision fatigue and focus on exceptions.

Without automation, Relay feels like a constant speed race and you'll miss good freight. With it, Relay becomes a manageable, repeatable system.

Final Answer: Is It Worth It?

For volume-focused carriers with strong on-time performance and access to Amazon facilities: Yes. The steady work and fast pay are valuable.

For carriers who need rate negotiation, flexibility, or long-haul work: No. You'll be frustrated by Relay's fixed pricing and regional focus.

For carriers who are currently on Relay but frustrated: Likely yes, but only if you adopt automation. An auto-refresh tool and rate/profit intelligence can make Relay easier to manage.

Frequently asked questions

Do most carriers make money on Amazon Relay?

Amazon doesn't publish carrier profit data, and we don't either. Whether it works depends on your costs, location, equipment and discipline. Your net profit per load, after driver pay, fuel, maintenance and deadhead, is what decides it.

Can I run Relay part-time?

Yes. You set your availability and accept or decline loads as you choose. But the acceptance metric tracks cancellations of loads you booked, so book only what you can run.

What's the biggest pain point for Relay carriers?

The speed competition for good loads and rate variability are the two biggest complaints. Refreshers and booking rules help with speed, and lane history and net profit help with judging rates.

Should I quit my broker and switch to Relay full-time?

Not necessarily. Relay can be one channel alongside other freight. Amazon's freight is only on Relay, but not every lane or shipper is Amazon.

Is Relay harder to manage than a traditional freight board?

No, it's actually simpler. With a freight board, you hunt for loads and negotiate. With Relay, loads are published, spot prices are listed, and invoices are automatic. The trade-off is less control and less negotiation.

How do I know if I have the right equipment for Relay?

Check the equipment requirements. Day cab, sleeper, box truck (16/26 ft with liftgate), reefer, dry van and containers all work. Cargo vans don't.
